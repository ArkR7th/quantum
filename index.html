Chapter 01

Foundations of
Quantum Computing

Classical computers process information as bits — discrete values of 0 or 1. Every operation, from rendering a pixel to encrypting a file, reduces to sequences of these binary states. The transistors inside your processor are switches: either on or off, conducting or blocking.

Quantum computing replaces bits with qubits — quantum mechanical systems whose state is described not by a single binary value, but by a complex-valued probability amplitude. Rather than being forced into 0 or 1, a qubit can exist in a superposition of both simultaneously, until the moment of measurement forces a definite outcome.

This distinction is not merely philosophical. It enables quantum algorithms to explore exponentially many computational paths in parallel. Grover's algorithm searches an unsorted database of N items in O(√N) steps. Shor's algorithm factors large integers in polynomial time — a problem believed to require exponential time classically, underpinning modern cryptographic infrastructure.

The mathematical framework is linear algebra over the complex numbers. States are unit vectors in a Hilbert space. Operations are unitary matrices. Measurement is projection. This machinery, initially developed to describe electrons and photons, turns out to be the most natural language for describing reversible, interference-capable computation.

The Qubit State

A general qubit state is written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, where α and β are complex amplitudes satisfying |α|² + |β|² = 1. The probabilities of measuring 0 or 1 are |α|² and |β|² respectively.

Hilbert Space

An n-qubit system lives in a 2ⁿ-dimensional complex Hilbert space. With just 300 qubits, the state vector has more components than there are atoms in the observable universe.

Unitarity

All quantum gates are unitary: U†U = I. This ensures quantum evolution is reversible and probability is conserved — a fundamental constraint with no classical analogue.

⚛

Qubit

The fundamental unit of quantum information. Physically realized in the spin of an electron, the polarization of a photon, or the energy level of a superconducting circuit. Mathematically, a unit vector in ℂ².

∿

Amplitude

Complex numbers encoding both the probability and the phase of each computational basis state. Crucially, amplitudes can interfere — canceling or reinforcing — enabling quantum speedups.

◎

Measurement

Projecting the quantum state onto a classical outcome. The result is probabilistic and irreversible — the wavefunction collapses. The probabilities are the squared magnitudes of the corresponding amplitudes.

⊠

Unitary Evolution

Between measurements, quantum states evolve according to unitary transformations — the quantum analogue of classical logic gates. Unitarity preserves the total probability and guarantees reversibility.

Chapter 03

Quantum Gates
& Circuits

Quantum gates are unitary operations that transform qubit states. They are the quantum analogue of classical logic gates, but unlike AND and OR, all quantum gates are reversible. Select a gate to explore its matrix representation and effect.

Hadamard Gate

Creates an equal superposition from a basis state. Applying H to |0⟩ yields (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, the "+" state. Applying H twice returns the original state — it is its own inverse. The Hadamard is the workhorse of quantum algorithms, initializing registers into superposition and enabling interference.

Quantum
Entanglement

Entanglement is a uniquely quantum correlation between qubits. An entangled pair cannot be described by the states of its individual components — measuring one qubit instantly determines the outcome of measuring the other, regardless of physical distance.

Both qubits are in superposition, maximally entangled.

The Bell state |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 is the simplest example of maximal entanglement. Neither qubit has a definite value — the system exists in a superposition of "both 0" and "both 1". Yet the two outcomes are perfectly correlated.

Einstein famously called this "spooky action at a distance," believing it implied quantum mechanics was incomplete — that hidden variables must pre-determine the outcomes. Bell's theorem (1964) and subsequent experiments (Aspect 1982, Hensen 2015) ruled out local hidden-variable theories, confirming that entanglement is a genuine, irreducible feature of nature.

Entanglement is not a communication channel — it cannot transmit information faster than light. But it is a powerful resource for computation, enabling quantum teleportation, superdense coding, and error-correction protocols with no classical analogue.

Bell's Theorem

John Bell proved in 1964 that any local hidden-variable theory must satisfy certain inequalities. Quantum mechanics predicts — and experiments confirm — violations of these Bell inequalities, ruling out classical explanations.

Entanglement as Resource

Entanglement is consumed when used for tasks like quantum teleportation. It can be "distilled" from many weakly entangled pairs and is a quantifiable resource in quantum information theory.

Chapter 05

Quantum
Interference

Interference is the mechanism by which quantum algorithms achieve their speedups. Probability amplitudes — unlike probabilities — can be negative or complex, and thus can cancel. A well-designed algorithm amplifies amplitudes of correct answers and suppresses wrong ones.

Constructive Interference

When amplitudes add in phase, the combined amplitude grows. Quantum algorithms engineer circuits so that computational paths leading to the correct answer interfere constructively, increasing its measurement probability.

✧

Destructive Interference

Amplitudes that are out of phase cancel. This suppresses wrong answers. Grover's algorithm, Shor's algorithm, and most quantum speedups depend critically on this cancellation of unwanted computational paths.
